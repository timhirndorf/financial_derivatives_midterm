PART A - HOW THE PROFESSOR'S LECTURES ARE BUILT (and how we copy it)
=====================================================================
Observed in Options, Replication_One_Period, Continuous, Hedging, Numerical,
Elements Stochastic Calculus:

1. Fixed frame. Each lecture has the same three opening slides:
     (1) title + "Riccardo Rebonato",
     (2) "Outline" (the list of sections),
     (3) "Plan of the Lecture" (4-6 bullets starting with verbs: "Show...",
         "Explain...", "Invoke no-arbitrage to...", "Show what the result
         does not depend on").
   -> We do the same: Title, Outline, "Plan of the Presentation".

2. The slide title is the SECTION name, repeated on every slide of the section
   ("Hedging", "Hedging", ... / "Two Steps", "Two Steps", ...). There are no
   catchy per-slide headlines.
   -> Our section titles are Pricing / Replication / Greeks / Conclusions.

3. Plain LaTeX Beamer, default theme, white background, triangle bullets
   (the ▶ marker), numbered equations (1), (2), ... Figures are captioned
   "Figure: ...". No colour, no icons, no logos.
   -> Build the deck in Beamer (or a plain PowerPoint copying that look) and
      number the equations so we can say "from equation (4)...".

4. Text is prose: full sentences, 40-90 words per slide, one idea per slide.
   Short sentences, first person ("I want to build a portfolio...",
   "Let's see:").

5. Proofs are written as "Proof:" followed by numbered steps (1., 2., 3.) and
   end with the conclusion. They are mostly no-arbitrage or Ito/Taylor
   arguments, never heavy measure theory. When he skips something he says so
   ("one can show - we won't - that...").

6. Intuition first, maths second, then a remark on what the result DOES NOT
   depend on (the real-world drift mu disappears; investors' risk preferences
   do not matter). This is his favourite point.

7. He asks the room questions, then answers on the next line or slide
   ("Can you think of two examples...?" / "Answer: ...", "What must mu be?",
   "How can this work?").

8. He links back to earlier results ("Remember how we derived...",
   "Remember the Ito isometry").

9. He uses informal asides and a bit of humour ("mercifully and magically
   cancel out", "Mr Ito would not be happy", "just nod encouragingly").

10. He frames things by how practitioners think: hedging is about delivered
    variance; volatility is the "BIG problem".

Design consequence for 12 minutes: the frame slides take almost no time, the
"Plan" slide does the roadmap, and each section gets 2-3 content slides. We
include only proofs that he would recognise (Ito / Taylor / no-arbitrage /
CLT). Everything else goes to the backup slides.

SLIDE 4 - Pricing  (one price, four engines)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
TEXT
  Remember that, given dS_t = mu S_t dt + sigma S_t dz_t, no-arbitrage gives
  the PDE
     dC/dt + r S dC/dS + 1/2 sigma^2 S^2 d2C/dS2 - r C = 0             (1)
  and, by Feynman-Kac, its solution is the discounted expectation
     C = e^{-rT} E^Q[ max(S_T - K, 0) ],
     S_T = S0 exp[(r - 1/2 sigma^2) T + sigma sqrt(T) eps]            (2)
  ▶ Black-Scholes solves (1) in closed form:
     C = S0 N(h1) - K e^{-rT} N(h2), h1 = 0.297, h2 = 0.097
  ▶ Finite differences solve (1) on a grid.
  ▶ Monte Carlo and the tree compute (2), by sampling and by enumerating.
VISUAL   none (or a small 2-arrow sketch: Expectation <-> PDE)
PROOF    none on the slide. We "remember" the derivation from the lecture
         ("Black and Scholes in Continuous Time").
BEHIND   Ito on C, Delta = dC/dS kills dz, the riskless portfolio earns r,
         mu drops out (v1 slide 3). Exact lognormal sampling means MC has no
         time-discretisation bias.
SAY      "All four methods compute the same number, so they must agree. What
          differs is speed and the type of error."

SLIDE 6 - Pricing  (convergence)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
TEXT
  ▶ Monte Carlo: error ~ 1/sqrt(M). Variance of a mean of M i.i.d. payoffs
    = s^2/M (CLT).
  ▶ Tree: error ~ 1/N. The binomial distribution of log S_T tends to a
    Gaussian. The error zig-zags because the kink at K falls on or between
    nodes (odd/even N).
  ▶ Crank-Nicolson: error ~ 1/N^2. Central differences in S and in t
    (Taylor: the odd terms cancel).
  10x more effort gives 3.2x / 10x / 100x smaller error.
VISUAL   three log-log error plots side by side (notebook: MC SE, tree error,
         FD error) with slopes -1/2, -1, -2.
         Caption: "Figure: Pricing error vs. effort (log-log)."
PROOF    on the slide: just the three one-line reasons above.
         Optional line: error = c N^{-k} => log error = log c - k log N,
         a straight line with slope -k.
BEHIND   Full arguments in backup B3 (Taylor), B4 (MC SE) and v1 slide 7
         (CRR, CLT). Numbers: MC SE 1.0 / 0.014 / 0.0014 at M = 10^2 / 10^6
         / 10^8; tree error 1.8 / 0.02 / 0.002 / 0.0002 at N = 1 / 10^2 /
         10^3 / 10^4; FD error divides by 4 with every doubling.
SAY      "Same answer, very different speed. The slope of the line is the
          order of convergence."

SLIDE 7 - Pricing  (uncertainty)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
TEXT
  ▶ Monte Carlo error is statistical: random and unbiased. We report a
    confidence interval, C_hat +/- 1.96 SE.
  ▶ Tree and grid errors are discretisation errors: deterministic, a bias
    with no confidence interval, and known from the convergence order.
  ▶ Which error is the largest?
  ▶ Answer: none of the above. A 1 vol-point error in sigma moves the price
    by vega x 0.01 = 0.38, about 200 times the MC standard error.
PROOF    Error = bias + noise. MC: bias 0, noise s/sqrt(M). Tree/FD: bias
         c N^{-k}, noise 0. Model error ~ Vega x (sigma_true - sigma_used).
SAY      "Our numerical errors are tiny. The real uncertainty is the
          volatility we put in." (This sets up Replication.)

SLIDE 9 - Replication  (the proof: gamma vs. theta)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
TEXT
  Proof: P&L of the hedged position over one step dt
  1. Taylor/Ito: dC = Theta dt + Delta dS + 1/2 Gamma dS^2
  2. The replicating portfolio earns Delta dS + r (C - Delta S) dt
  3. The PDE (1) gives -Theta + r (C - Delta S) = 1/2 sigma^2 S^2 Gamma
  4. Therefore
       d eps = 1/2 Gamma S^2 [ sigma^2 dt - (dS/S)^2 ]                (3)
     "variance we were paid for" minus "variance that was delivered"
  ▶ E[(dS/S)^2] = sigma^2 dt, so the mean error is 0.
  ▶ Each step adds noise of order dt; summed over N steps, std ~ 1/sqrt(N).
  ▶ mu does not appear in (3): the result does not depend on the drift.
VISUAL   optional: the "break-even movements" picture style from Hedging.pdf
         (option parabola above the delta line)
BEHIND
  - As dt -> 0, (dS)^2 -> sigma^2 S^2 dt exactly (Ito isometry: "there are
    no lucky paths"), so eps -> 0. This is the Black-Scholes argument itself,
    and "Gamma = -Theta" from the Hedging lecture.
  - Check against a known formula (Derman-Kamal 1999):
    std(eps) ~ sqrt(pi/4) sigma Vega / sqrt(N) = 6.77/sqrt(N)
    gives 3.38 / 1.95 / 0.94 / 0.43 / 0.21 vs. our 3.22 / 1.94 / 0.95 /
    0.44 / 0.22. Backup B1.
SAY      "This is the key slide. After delta hedging, what is left is a bet
          on volatility: gamma times implied minus realised variance."

---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 10 - Replication  (what it depends on, when it fails)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
TEXT
  Equation (3) tells us everything:
  ▶ Rebalancing frequency: std ~ 1/sqrt(N). Quarterly gives 3.2, about a
    third of the premium.
  ▶ Where the path goes: Gamma S^2 is largest near K close to expiry, so
    paths ending near the strike have the biggest errors.
  ▶ The volatility that was delivered:
      True vol     10%     15%     20%    25%     30%
      Mean eps     +3.90   +1.97   0.01   -1.98   -3.97
    Check: Vega x 5 vol points = 38.17 x 0.05 = 1.91.
  ▶ Not the drift: mean about 0 for mu = 0%, 5%, 10%, 20%.
  Works well with frequent trading, the right sigma and no jumps. Fails with
  rare trading, wrong sigma, S near K near expiry, transaction costs, jumps.
VISUAL   scatter "eps vs. S_T" (funnel at K) or the wrong-vol histograms.
         Pick ONE.
PROOF    Taking expectations in (3) with sigma_real != sigma:
         E[eps] = 1/2 * integral of E[Gamma S^2] (sigma^2 - sigma_real^2) dt,
         which is about Vega x (sigma - sigma_real).
SAY      "Wrong volatility turns noise into a systematic loss. In the
          lecture's words: the variance delivered is the BIG problem."

SLIDE 11 - Greeks  (results)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
TEXT
            BS        MC (10^6, SE)       Tree (N = 1000)
  Delta     0.6168    0.6167 (0.0006)     0.6167
  Gamma     0.01909   0.01903 (0.00011)   0.01910
  Vega      38.17     38.19 (0.08)        38.16
  ▶ The option is at the money. Why is delta 0.62 and not 0.5?
  ▶ Answer: h1 = (r + 1/2 sigma^2) sqrt(T) / sigma = 0.297 > 0. The forward
    S0 e^{rT} = 104 > K, and the lognormal is right-skewed. Delta is a hedge
    ratio, not the probability of exercise (N(h2) = 0.54).
PROOF    closed forms Delta = N(h1), Gamma = phi(h1)/(S sigma sqrt T),
         Vega = S phi(h1) sqrt T. The "magic cancellation" is
         S phi(h1) = K e^{-rT} phi(h2) (backup B2).
SAY      "All three methods agree to 3-4 digits."

---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 12 - Greeks  (how each method gets them)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
TEXT
  ▶ Tree: delta and gamma come for free from the first nodes
    (Delta = (C_u - C_d)/(Su - Sd), as in one-period replication). Vega
    needs two extra trees. Error ~ 1/N.
  ▶ Monte Carlo: bump and reprice with the same random numbers,
      Delta ~ [C(S+h) - C(S-h)]/(2h),
      Gamma ~ [C(S+h) - 2C(S) + C(S-h)]/h^2
    Bias ~ h^2, but the variance of gamma ~ 1/h: only paths ending near K
    contribute. That is a bias-variance trade-off, and we use h = 1.
VISUAL   "MC gamma vs. bump size h" (notebook)
BEHIND   Common random numbers: Var(X - Y) = Var X + Var Y - 2 Cov(X,Y).
         Pathwise and likelihood-ratio estimators exist (mention if asked).
SAY      "The tree gets Greeks almost for free. Monte Carlo has to difference
          noisy numbers, and gamma suffers most."

---------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 13 - Conclusions
---------------------------------------------------------------------
TEXT
  ▶ All engines converge to 9.894: MC at order 1/2, the tree at order 1,
    Crank-Nicolson at order 2.
  ▶ The numerical error is small compared with the error from the volatility
    input (vega).
  ▶ The delta-hedging error is 1/2 Gamma S^2 (implied - realised variance).
    With the right sigma it is zero on average and shrinks like 1/sqrt(N).
    With the wrong sigma it is a systematic P&L of about Vega x the vol error.
  ▶ The result does not depend on the drift mu.
  ▶ Gamma and vega peak near the strike, and that is where hedging is
    hardest.
SAY      end with: "Questions?"

PART D - BACKUP SLIDES (after Conclusions, only for Q&A)
=====================================================================
B1 Replication - discrete-hedging error vs. Derman-Kamal
   Table: simulated std vs. 6.77/sqrt(N) for N = 4, 12, 52, 252, 1000.
B2 Greeks - Delta = N(h1)
   Proof: phi(h2)/phi(h1) = exp((h1^2 - h2^2)/2) = S e^{rT}/K,
   so S phi(h1) = K e^{-rT} phi(h2), so the extra terms cancel.
B3 Pricing - finite-difference truncation error
   Taylor: V(S +/- h); subtract to get V' + O(h^2), add to get V'' + O(h^2).
   CN is centred in time, so O(dt^2). CN is unconditionally stable; the
   explicit scheme in the lecture is not ("can be unstable").
B4 Pricing - MC standard error and CI
   Var(mean) = s^2/M, CLT, 95% CI. s is about 14.
B5 Pricing - CRR tree
   u = e^{sigma sqrt dt}, d = 1/u, p = (e^{r dt} - d)/(u - d).
   Matches mean and variance (as in "A Twist in the Tale"). The odd/even
   oscillation comes from the strike sitting on or between nodes.
